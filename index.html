01

Learning to See

Before any strategy, I needed to understand what a model actually is. So I trained one by hand.

NEURAL NETS

Not intelligent, just tracking

I labeled images "cat" or "not cat" and watched the network's weights shift with each example. The model is not intelligent in any meaningful sense. It tracks which features show up with which label, and more examples make the tracking better. When it failed on a tricky image, the lesson was clear: example quality matters as much as quantity.

CNN

What "deep" actually means

I watched a four-stage visualization turn a pedestrian photo into a confident classification. Pixels became edges, edges became body parts, body parts became a person. Nobody tells each layer what to look for. It discovers the features on its own. That layering is what makes it deep.

KEY THREAD

The long-tail problem

A few common scenarios dominate driving data. Thousands of rare ones, a costumed pedestrian, debris on the road, a wheelchair behind a parked truck, barely appear. AI products fail in that gap between the common and the rare. It became the thread running through every step that followed.

03

The World Model

To beat the long-tail problem, Waymo uses a world model: AI trained on reality that generates driving scenarios nobody ever recorded.

HOW IT WORKS

Scenarios from ingredients

I built rare scenarios by composing four ingredients. The model assembles each one from learned knowledge, not recorded footage.

Heavy rainNightWheelchair userParked truck
CyclistAgainst trafficConstruction zoneDusk
THE LEAP

50 million miles, in seconds

The simulator estimated it would take roughly 50 million miles of real driving to encounter those scenarios at meaningful frequency. The world model generated them in seconds. Built on Genie 3 through transfer learning, it already understood rain, night, occlusion, and construction from general video. It needed no driving-specific recordings.

Try it mentally: a wheelchair user emerging from behind a parked truck during heavy rain at night. No fleet on Earth has that footage. The world model renders it on demand.

To: Lance (VP Strategy)  ·  Subject: World Model Investment

Hi Lance,

I want to make the case that our world model investment is a competitive advantage, not just an R&D line item.

Here is the core argument. Fleet driving scales with miles. Rare scenarios do not. Even Tesla's 6 million cars collecting data every day cannot reliably surface events like a wheelchair user emerging from behind a parked truck during heavy rain at night, or a cyclist riding against traffic through a construction zone at dusk. I built both of those scenarios in our simulator in seconds. To encounter them naturally at meaningful frequency would require roughly 50 million miles of real world driving. We generated them on demand.

Tesla's volume advantage is real and I would not dismiss it. But volume solves the head of the distribution, the everyday stuff. The long tail is what kills deployments, and no amount of passive fleet data closes that gap efficiently. Waymo's world model, built on Genie 3 through transfer learning, can compose scenarios from ingredients it already understands without needing a single recorded example of that exact situation.

If we stop investing here we do not just slow down. We hand over the one advantage Tesla cannot replicate by selling more cars.

THE TAKEAWAY

Moving the sliders and watching each fix create a new problem made the alignment problem feel real. You cannot ask a model to maximize everything at once. You pick your priorities, document the tradeoff, and stay ready to retune.

✉Artifact: Safety Board Defense, Reward FunctionClick to read the full defense▾

The reward function I am bringing to this board was designed around one priority above all others: keeping the car out of situations it cannot recover from. I set the completion reward at +8 and distributed the full 20 penalty points with collision weighted heaviest at 12, close calls at 6, and wait time at just 2.

On the 300-attempt test set the policy hit 288 successful turns, clearing the 285 completion bar. It produced 1 collision, meeting the safety threshold. Average wait time came in at 9.2 seconds, which I will be direct about: that misses the under 8 second target. That is the tradeoff I am explicitly accepting, and I want to explain why I think it is the right one.

By keeping the wait penalty low the car learned to hold for gaps it was confident about rather than forcing turns under ambiguity. The result is a system that is conservative at unprotected lefts, which is exactly the behavior we should want, given that a collision at that maneuver carries consequences that a 1.2 second average delay simply does not.

I am not presenting this as a perfect result. I am presenting it as a defensible one, and I am prepared to defend the reasoning behind every number in that penalty budget.

05

Simulation-First vs Fleet-First

I allocated a $1B Waymo data budget across five categories, and watched how each mix builds a fundamentally different company over five years.

Simulation capacity35%
Fleet expansion30%
Human labelers15%
Sensor upgrades10%
Data partnerships10%

To test whether I had internalized the three evaluation questions, I applied them to Tesla.

Where does their data come from?

Directly from customers, whether they know it or not. Every Autopilot vehicle uploads camera feeds, interventions, and disengagements. In shadow mode the AI predicts silently and Tesla compares against what the human did. Six million cars across dozens of countries is coverage no purpose-built fleet can match.

Does the pipeline compound?

It compounds, but passively. More cars means more data, better models, more buyers. The catch: Tesla cannot decide on a Tuesday morning that it needs more icy intersections at dawn. It waits for the world to produce them, so rare scenarios accumulate slowly even at massive scale.

Can money alone replicate it?

Not quickly. You would need millions of vehicles running for a decade minimum. But Waymo shows you can route around the problem: targeted simulation closes the gap on the long tail specifically, which is exactly where the passive approach is weakest.

VERDICT

Medium to strong moat. Tesla's fleet data is genuinely hard to replicate at scale, but the passive way it compounds leaves the long tail exposed to simulation-first competitors who manufacture rare scenarios on demand instead of waiting for the world to produce them.

Picking City #5

I analyzed three real social-license incidents, protest coning in San Francisco, a blocked fire truck in Austin, taxi-union opposition in Miami, then weighed four candidate cities.

RECOMMENDED

Seattle

Tech-friendly regulators, no organized opposition yet. Rain and hills stress test the Dataset C model in ways that compound into advantage for every rain-heavy city after.

BLOCKED

New York

Expansion already blocked by the Transport Workers Union earlier in 2026. No path without a labor fight.

RISKY

Atlanta

Active NHTSA investigation into school bus violations, with lawmakers calling for a pause.

FRAGILE

Miami

Less organized opposition, but hurricane-season incidents would define the public narrative fast.

Recommendation: Launch city 5 in Seattle, and move now while the regulatory environment is still favorable and before a competitor establishes the Pacific Northwest narrative first.

Tech readiness. Seattle's constant rain, steep hills, and frequent construction zones are exactly the conditions that expose weak perception models, and that is precisely why it is the right environment for us right now. We are launching with Dataset C behind us, trained specifically on the rare compounding scenarios other datasets miss. Seattle will stress test our system in ways Phoenix and Austin never could, and that stress makes the model stronger for every city after it.

Data moat growth. Our $1B allocation puts 35% into simulation and 30% into fleet expansion, deliberately building the advantage Tesla cannot replicate. Seattle generates weather edge cases that are genuinely hard to manufacture even in simulation. Real rain on real hills, feeding a dataset that compounds in ways passive fleet collection cannot match. That coverage transfers directly to Portland, Vancouver, and Chicago.

Social license path. Seattle is winnable on community trust in a way New York and Atlanta are not right now. We engage transit unions, disability advocates, and bike safety groups before a single car hits the road, co-designing operating rules with them rather than presenting rules after the fact. Limited area pilot, transparent safety reporting, third party oversight, expansion only after jointly agreed benchmarks.

The risk I am accepting. Seattle's weather will surface edge cases our model has not seen, and some of those moments will be public. I accept that risk because our Dataset C investment and simulation coverage give us a stronger foundation than any competitor in similar conditions right now. Mitigation is proactive transparency: monthly safety reports, real data for oversight partners, and every public hesitation treated as a retraining opportunity rather than a crisis.

What I am watching in the first six months. Community partnerships holding through the first incidents. Edge case performance in rain staying within simulation predicted ranges. No regulatory escalation. Two early warnings would trigger recalibration: average wait times in complex intersections creeping above 9 seconds consistently, or a single partner organization publicly withdrawing from oversight.
